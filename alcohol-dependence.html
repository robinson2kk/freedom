Alcohol Dependence: How to Get Safe, Professional Help

If alcohol is affecting health, relationships, work or your ability to control drinking, support is available. The safest next step depends on how much and how often you drink, your health and whether your body may be physically dependent on alcohol.

Updated 2026-11-24 · General educational information · Not clinically reviewed

Safety comes first: If you drink heavily or may be physically dependent on alcohol, do not suddenly stop or attempt an unsupervised detox. Withdrawal can be dangerous and sometimes life-threatening. Contact a doctor or hospital to plan a medically supported reduction or withdrawal.

1. Notice signs that drinking needs attention

Possible warning signs include repeatedly drinking more than intended, unsuccessful attempts to cut down, strong urges, needing more alcohol for the same effect, withdrawal symptoms when alcohol wears off, or continuing despite harm to health, relationships or responsibilities. Only a qualified professional can assess and diagnose alcohol use disorder or dependence.

2. Speak honestly with a healthcare professional

A doctor can ask about drinking patterns, previous withdrawal symptoms, other health conditions and medicines. This information helps determine whether withdrawal needs medical monitoring and what treatment options may be appropriate. You do not need to wait for a crisis to ask for help.

3. Understand why detox should be supervised

In someone with physical alcohol dependence, suddenly stopping can cause withdrawal symptoms such as shaking, sweating, nausea, anxiety and insomnia. Severe withdrawal can involve seizures, hallucinations or delirium and requires urgent medical care. Do not try to manage severe symptoms alone.

4. Treatment can continue after withdrawal

Withdrawal management is only one part of care. Ongoing treatment may include structured psychological support, counselling, medicines prescribed by a clinician, peer support and help addressing health or social needs. WHO guidance includes psychosocial interventions for alcohol dependence; a professional can help select an approach that fits the person.

5. Prepare for the first conversation

  • Write down how often and how much you usually drink.
  • Note when you last drank and any symptoms when you delay or stop.
  • Mention previous withdrawal, seizures, hallucinations or hospital visits.
  • List medicines, other substances and health conditions.
  • Ask what to do if symptoms begin and where to get urgent help.

6. Find support in India

For immediate danger or severe symptoms such as seizures, confusion or hallucinations, call India's emergency number 112 or go to the nearest emergency department. FREEDOM's Immediate Help page lists India-based emergency and mental-health support contacts. For non-emergency care, contact a doctor, psychiatrist or addiction-treatment service.

You do not have to do this alone

Asking for help is a constructive first step. If you are supporting someone else, listen without blame and encourage them to speak with a healthcare professional. Do not pressure someone to detox at home.
