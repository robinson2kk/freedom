Understanding cravings and urges

An urge can feel intense, distracting or convincing. It is an experience—not an instruction. You can pause, notice what is happening and choose your next step without judging yourself.

FREEDOM Editorial Team · Published 21 November 2026 · Educational information, not clinical advice

First, remember this

A craving does not mean you have failed or that you must act on it. Urges can rise and fall. Their pattern and intensity vary between people and behaviours, so there is no guaranteed countdown for an urge to disappear.

1. Notice what may be triggering it

Triggers can include a place, person, time of day, device, memory, emotion or routine. Stress, boredom, loneliness and tiredness may also make an urge harder to manage. Sometimes the trigger is not obvious.

Instead of blaming yourself, ask: What was happening just before this urge appeared? A simple note can help you recognise patterns over time.

2. Create a small pause

You do not need to solve your whole recovery journey in one moment. Try a short pause before making a decision:

Step 1 — Name it

Say to yourself: “I'm noticing an urge right now.”

Step 2 — Ground yourself

Take a few comfortable breaths and notice your feet on the floor. Look around and name a few things you can see.

Step 3 — Change the setting

Move to another room, step outside, put the triggering device away or leave a high-risk situation if it is safe to do so.

Step 4 — Choose one alternative

Take a short walk, drink water, do a simple task or contact someone supportive.

Step 5 — Review without judgement

When the moment has passed, note what helped and what you might try next time.

3. Prepare a personal urge plan

Make your plan while you feel relatively calm. Keep it simple enough to use when concentration is difficult.

  • My common trigger: What situation, feeling or routine often comes before an urge?
  • My first action: What is one safe action I can take immediately?
  • My support person: Who can I contact without feeling judged?
  • My environment change: What can I move, block, leave or prepare in advance?

4. If you have a setback

A setback does not erase the effort you have already made. When you are ready, look at what happened with curiosity rather than punishment. What was the trigger? What made the behaviour easier to access? What support or change could help next time?

If the behaviour feels difficult to control, is affecting your health or relationships, or keeps disrupting daily life, consider speaking with a qualified health professional. Support may include counselling, structured treatment or medical care depending on the issue.

Important safety note: If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, do not suddenly stop or reduce heavy drinking without medical advice. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous and may require supervised treatment. Seek urgent medical help for severe withdrawal symptoms, seizures, confusion or hallucinations.
