Managing Pornography Use When It Feels Out of Control

People have different relationships with pornography. Watching pornography does not automatically mean someone has a disorder. If your use feels difficult to control or is affecting your wellbeing, relationships, work or daily life, you can explore change without shame.

Updated 2026-11-24 · General educational information · Not clinically reviewed

Start with curiosity, not labels

Ask what you want to change and why. Is the concern time spent online, distress, conflict with personal values, interference with sleep or responsibilities, or repeated unsuccessful attempts to change? Be specific about the impact rather than diagnosing yourself.

1. Notice the pattern

For a short period, privately note the context around urges: time of day, mood, stress, boredom, loneliness, device, location and what happened just before. Avoid recording explicit details. The goal is to understand patterns, not to judge yourself.

2. Decide on a realistic boundary

  • Choose device-free places or times, such as the bedroom or the first part of your morning.
  • Keep your phone away from your bed or charge it outside the bedroom if practical.
  • Use content filters or website blockers as optional friction, not as a substitute for support.
  • Plan an alternative activity for times when you are most likely to browse automatically.

3. Respond to an urge

Pause, name what you are experiencing, change your setting and choose one next action. You might take a walk, move to a shared space, start a planned task or contact someone you trust. See how to manage cravings and try the Beat the Urge tool.

4. Understand the clinical distinction

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 includes compulsive sexual behaviour disorder as a specific impulse-control disorder. That does not mean all pornography use, masturbation or high sexual interest is a disorder. A qualified clinician considers persistent difficulty controlling behaviour and significant impairment or distress in context. Distress based only on moral disapproval is not, by itself, enough to establish the diagnosis.

5. When to seek support

Consider speaking with a qualified mental-health professional if you repeatedly feel unable to control the behaviour, it is interfering with daily life or relationships, or distress is difficult to manage. A clinician can help assess what is happening and discuss appropriate approaches. FREEDOM's professional directory may help you explore support options.

No shame, no assumptions: This article is educational and cannot diagnose compulsive sexual behaviour disorder. Seek a clinician who approaches sexual health respectfully and without stigma.
